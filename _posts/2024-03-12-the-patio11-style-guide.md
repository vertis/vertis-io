    - Break complex topics down into their component parts. Guide the reader through your reasoning step by step.
    - Use short paragraphs and subheadings to make your writing easy to scan and navigate.
    - Conclude with a clear call to action or takeaway.
3. Explanation and Argumentation:
    
    - Use concrete examples and analogies to illustrate abstract concepts.
    - Anticipate and address counterarguments or common misconceptions.
    - Back up your points with data, anecdotes, or case studies where appropriate.
    - Use rhetorical questions to engage the reader and provoke thought.
4. Sentence Structure:
    
    - Favor clear, direct sentences. Avoid unnecessary jargon or obscure words.
    - Use short, punchy sentences for emphasis. Mix sentence lengths for variety and rhythm.
    - Employ parallelism and repetition strategically to drive points home.
    - Don't be afraid of starting sentences with conjunctions like "But" or "And" for effect.
5. Word Choice and Terminology:
    
    - Use domain-specific terminology when appropriate, but always explain it for a general audience.
    - Choose vivid, specific verbs over vague or generic ones.
    - Opt for simple, Anglo-Saxon words over complex Latinate ones when possible.
    - Coin memorable phrases or terms when needed to encapsulate key concepts.
6. Formatting and Presentation:
    
    - Use bold or italic text sparingly for emphasis.
    - Break up long blocks of text with images, charts, or other visuals when appropriate.
    - Employ bulleted or numbered lists to make information easy to scan and absorb.
    - Link generously to relevant resources or additional reading.

Remember, the key is to be clear, direct, and engaging. Aim to inform and persuade your reader, but also to provoke thought and spark discussion. With McKenzie as your model, your writing should be both substantive and approachable, data-driven and anecdotally rich, serious in its intent but lighthearted in its delivery.
