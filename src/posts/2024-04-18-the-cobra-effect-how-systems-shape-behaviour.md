I've always been fascinated by human behavior, especially within the context of teams and organizations. Over the years, I've come to realize that while individual personalities and skills certainly matter, the systems we create often play a far greater role in shaping how people act. When a single person exhibits a certain behavior, we might attribute it to their individual personality or choices. However, when multiple people within a system consistently behave in the same way, it's a strong indicator that the system itself is influencing their actions.

A classic example of this phenomenon is the “cobra effect,” a term used to describe unintended consequences arising from poorly designed incentives. The name originates from an anecdote set in colonial India, where the British government, concerned about the number of venomous cobras in Delhi, offered a bounty for every dead cobra. Initially, this was successful in reducing the cobra population. However, enterprising individuals soon began breeding cobras for the income, ultimately increasing the cobra problem. When the government realized this, they scrapped the reward program, causing the breeders to release their now-worthless cobras, further exacerbating the situation.

This principle of unintended consequences played out in a company I worked for where the management wanted account managers to encourage clients to complete their online profiles. To incentivize this, they tied commissions to profile completion rates. However, instead of engaging with clients and explaining the benefits of completing profiles, many account managers simply filled them out themselves. The desire for complete profiles was understandable, but the chosen method created a system that led to an undesirable outcome.

These experiences, along with many others, have shaped my approach to leadership and team dynamics. I've learned that it's crucial to look beyond individual actions and consider the systems and incentives that drive those actions. As leaders, we have a responsibility to design systems that encourage collaboration, creativity, and ethical behavior. This means:

- Aligning incentives with desired outcomes: Instead of focusing solely on metrics, we need to consider the behaviors we want to promote and design incentives that support those behaviors.
- Creating transparency and open communication: When people understand the rationale behind goals and incentives, they're more likely to act in ways that benefit the entire team and organization.
- Fostering a culture of trust and psychological safety: People should feel comfortable speaking up when they see problems with the system or its incentives.
- Continuously evaluating and adapting systems: No system is perfect, and what works today may not work tomorrow. Leaders need to be vigilant in monitoring outcomes and making adjustments as needed.

By understanding the power of systems and incentives, and by learning from examples like the cobra effect, we can create environments where individuals thrive, teams excel, and organizations achieve their goals in a sustainable and ethical manner.
